# RUN-007 Report — U.S. Care-delivery Influence Map

Last updated: 2026-07-15 01:25:00 PDT — edited by: Codex

## Result

Run 007 produced a 50-record public influence registry across hospital and health-system media, behavioral-health sources, FQHC/community-health channels, rural and post-acute care, healthcare workforce media, nursing podcasts, and clinician/creator channels. Forty-five records have direct page-opened evidence and five are retained as search-snippet-only leads. The result is a research shortlist—not a contact list, buyer list, endorsement list, or completeness claim.

The strongest first learning route is a layered channel strategy: begin with specialist operator and workforce sources, then use larger healthcare publications for evidence-backed category education. Consumer mental-health creators are useful for language and awareness research but are not equivalent to care-delivery distribution channels.

## What the sweep suggests

- **Specialists outperform generic reach for learning.** The most decision-useful sources repeatedly name a care setting, operator audience, workforce issue, or delivery constraint.
- **Workforce is a cross-segment bridge.** Nursing, HR, behavioral-health, home-health, hospice, and hospital sources all discuss staffing, retention, burnout, leadership, or caregiver support, but they do not imply that one intervention fits every setting.
- **Podcast is the common small-source format.** Many niche sources use recurring interviews with CEOs, clinical leaders, HR leaders, or frontline workers; this creates a useful future research route without assuming sponsorship or outreach.
- **Creators reach workers more than buyers.** Nurse and therapist creators can shape language and cultural relevance, but their public audience is generally workers, learners, patients, or consumers—not verified organizational decision-makers.
- **Large outlets require stronger evidence.** KFF Health News, STAT, Health Affairs, NEJM Catalyst, and top trade publications are better treated as evidence and category-education channels than as easy distribution opportunities.

## Contradictions and limits

- **Influence is not buying authority.** A source may reach care-delivery workers or leaders without having access to a budget owner.
- **Publisher reach is unevenly evidenced.** Some audience figures are first-party claims; many small sources publish no reliable reach metrics.
- **Association media is not independent journalism.** AHA, NACHC, NRHA, AACN, and similar channels may be highly relevant but are member- or advocacy-oriented.
- **Consumer creators are adjacent.** Kati Morton, Dr Tracey Marks, and Steph Anya are relevant to mental-health language but should not be treated as direct `SEG-001` channels without a separate audience-fit test.
- **Completeness is bounded.** This was a four-hour/50-record sweep; it did not claim to find every local journalist, newsletter, YouTube channel, TikTok account, LinkedIn creator, or regional association.

## Decision

**Advance to a narrower research step, not outreach.** The registry supports a future comparison of two channel paths:

1. `operator-specialist`: behavioral health, FQHC, hospital workforce, home health, hospice, and long-term-care sources;
2. `workforce-storytelling`: nursing, clinician, therapist, and frontline-worker creators/podcasts.

The next smallest useful question is: **Which 5–8 channels in one chosen care-delivery branch show current audience fit, recurring workforce/care-delivery coverage, and a plausible non-promotional learning format?**
